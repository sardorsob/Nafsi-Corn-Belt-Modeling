Abstract
We use three CropSmart data products, the USDA Cropland Data Layer (CDL), weekly MODIS NDVI, and
weekly SMAP Level-4 soil moisture, to study what is planted across the U.S. Corn Belt, how those crops develop
through the season, and the soil conditions they develop under. By fitting Hilbert space Gaussian processes to crop-
level NDVI, we find that corn, soybean, and winter wheat are separated by when their seasonal peak occurs rather
than how high it reaches. We identify corn-soybean rotation from ten-year CDL histories with a transparent rule, and
find that how much of the region counts as regularly rotated depends heavily on how strictly “regular” is defined. We
score soil moisture against its usual seasonal range with a conjugate Bayesian model suited to SMAP’s short record,
and contrast the wet 2019 planting season with the dry spell of summer 2022. Finally, we train a gradient boosted
classifier that combines crop history with current-season observations. Seasonal NDVI adds modestly to what crop
history already provides, soil moisture adds almost nothing, and the classifier is weakest on fields whose crop histories
are irregular. We discuss how these outputs could be combined, as well as the limitations of our implementation.

1 Introduction
Climate variability is an increasingly present threat to U.S. agriculture: shifting precipitation patterns, more frequent
flash droughts, and wet springs that delay or prevent planting all affect what can be grown, and when. Responding
to these threats requires knowing three things about any given field, namely what is planted there, how that crop is
developing through the season, and what conditions it is developing under. No single dataset answers all three. The
United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Cropland Data Layer (CDL) records the crop in each pixel once a
year, but says nothing about the season itself [8]. A vegetation index such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation
Index (NDVI) tracks canopy development week by week, but does not label the crop [2]. And soil moisture describes
the water available to the crop, but at a much coarser resolution than either of the other two [5].
The CropSmart digital twin makes all three of these products available as operational data streams, and the National
Food Security & Innovation (NAFSI) Track 1 challenge asks how they can be used together for crop monitoring.
Previous work has shown that each source is useful on its own: Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer
(MODIS) vegetation time series can separate crop types [9], and historical CDL sequences are strong predictors of
next year’s planting [10]. What is less clear is how much each source adds once the others are available. That is the
question that ties our four analyses together:
1. How do NDVI curves differ between corn, soybean, and winter wheat, and which features of those curves
separate the crops consistently across years?

2 Data
Our study area covers 13 Corn Belt states: Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri,
Nebraska, North Dakota, Ohio, South Dakota, and Wisconsin. We used three data streams, all accessed through
CropSmart:
1. USDA CDL: an annual, 30 m raster that assigns each pixel to one of more than 100 crop and land cover cate-
gories, produced by USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service (NASS) from satellite imagery and ground
reference data [8]. Each category has a numeric code - the ones we use most are corn (1), soybean (5), winter
wheat (24), winter wheat/soybean double crop (26), and oats (28). We used CDL years 2008-2025.
2. MODIS NDVI: a satellite measure of how green and dense the vegetation in a pixel is, ranging from about 0.1
for bare soil to above 0.9 for a full crop canopy [2]. Over a season, a crop’s NDVI rises as it leafs out, peaks
at full canopy, and falls as it matures. We used weekly 250 m composites covering approximately the 100th to
310th day of the year (DOY) - i.e., spring through mid-autumn.
3. SMAP L4 soil moisture: weekly surface soil moisture - i.e., the fraction of the topsoil’s volume that is water - on
a 9 km grid from NASA’s Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP) mission, which combines satellite observations
with a land surface model to produce estimates without gaps [5]. We used SMAP from 2015, the first full year
of the mission, onward.
These three products differ by more than two orders of magnitude in spatial resolution. A single SMAP cell spans
roughly 1,300 MODIS pixels and over 90,000 CDL pixels, which will matter for how we interpret both the moisture
analysis and its contribution to the classifier.
Because the three data streams come at very different resolutions, a single location on the ground is described by
one CDL pixel, a different MODIS pixel, and yet another SMAP pixel. To compare them, we placed all three on
a common grid of roughly 557 m cells,1 so that each grid cell carries a crop label, an NDVI time series, and a soil
moisture time series. Every analysis that follows works with these grid cells rather than with individual fields.
Two consequences of this are worth keeping in mind. First, placing data on a finer grid does not make it more
detailed: one 9 km SMAP value is simply copied to every grid cell inside it, so neighboring cells, and often neighboring
fields growing different crops, share the same soil moisture value. Second, we downloaded the data as a rectangle
around the 13 states, so a small share of cells (3.17% of those in the rotation analysis) lie just outside the state borders.
We keep them in the region-wide rotation results, but leave them out of any state-by-state summaries.

3 Methodology
3.1 Seasonal crop signatures
Corn and soybean can reach similar peak greenness, so a single NDVI snapshot taken at the height of summer may not
tell them apart. Our hypothesis going into Task 1 was that the crops are better separated by the shape and timing of
their seasonal curves - i.e., when they green up, how quickly, and when they peak. We added winter wheat as a third
crop because it follows an entirely different calendar: it is planted in the fall, overwinters, greens up in early spring,
and is harvested by midsummer, giving a cool-season contrast to the two warm-season row crops.
For each year from 2008 to 2025, we joined that year’s weekly NDVI to that year’s CDL labels and computed,
for every week, the spatial mean NDVI across all corn, soybean, and winter wheat cells (CDL codes 1, 5, and 24).
Using each year’s own CDL labels means that a cell can move between crop groups from year to year, which is exactly
what happens in a rotated field. Alongside the mean, we computed the 25th and 75th percentiles across cells for each
crop-week, giving an interquartile range (IQR) that describes how much greenness varies among fields in the same
3

week. We then converted each composite’s start date to DOY and pooled all years, giving 535 crop-week observations
per crop.

than imposing a fixed parametric shape on the season, we model NDVI as a smooth, latent function of DOY, t, using

However, GPs are notably bottlenecked by their computational expense, O(N3), where N denotes the number of

to those larger problems without being rebuilt.

The pooled model describes the average season. To ask which features separate the crops across years, we also
extracted three features from each crop’s raw weekly means in each year: the DOY of maximum NDVI, the maximum
NDVI itself, and the green-up slope, defined as the change in NDVI from the first observed week to the peak week,
expressed per 30 days.
3.2 Crop-history patterns
Corn-soybean rotation - i.e., alternating the two crops every year - is the most common rotation in the Midwest and the
canonical test case the challenge poses. And comparing multi-year CDL sequences is an established way to describe
rotation patterns [7]. Our goal was a criterion that is transparent, so that any cell’s label can be traced back to its ten-
year sequence, and that separates three behaviors: regular alternation, persistent single-crop cultivation (monoculture),
and everything else.
We considered the 2015-2024 CDL sequence of every cell that was corn or soybean in at least one year. A cell that
was grassland for eight years and corn for two does not carry enough information to say anything about its rotation, so

The thresholds encode a deliberately strict notion of “regular” - a cell may deviate from perfect alternation in at
most three of ten years and must spend at least seven years in corn or soybean. Because there is no ground truth
for what counts as “regular,” we also re-ran the classification over a grid of alternation thresholds (0.50 to 0.70) and

Annual CDL labels contain classification errors, and a single mislabeled year can move a cell from regular to
irregular even when the field around it rotates normally. To clean up these isolated labels, we smoothed the class map
with a majority filter: each cell looks at the 3 × 3 block of cells centered on it - i.e., itself and its eight immediate
neighbors, and takes on whichever class is most common in that block.6 The reasoning is that a rotation practice applies
to a whole field, which often spans more than one grid cell, so a lone cell that disagrees with all of its neighbors is
more likely a labeling error than a genuinely different field. However, smoothing can also change a cell’s label away
from what its own sequence says, so we report both the raw and smoothed shares.
The three classes summarize a cell’s whole decade in one label. That makes them easy to map, but it inherently hides
which year-to-year moves a history is made of: an irregular cell could be one that repeats corn, one that occasionally
grows wheat or alfalfa, or one with a single mislabeled year, and the class alone cannot tell these apart. To see which
moves are most common across the study area, we recoded every year of every eligible cell as corn, soybean, or other
(any other CDL class) and took each pair of consecutive years, 2015→2016 through 2023→2024, giving us nine pairs
per cell.

3.3 Wet and dry soil conditions
We examined two event windows chosen to contrast a wet and a dry season: April-July 2019, during the widespread
Midwest flooding and delayed planting, and June-August 2022, during the Great Plains dry spell. To keep the moisture
analysis aligned with the rotation analysis, we used the same eligible cells and labeled each cell with the CDL class
from its event year, since fields change crops between years and we wanted each summary to reflect what was actually
planted when the anomaly occurred. We report results for the crop strata of corn (1), soybean (5), winter wheat/soybean
double crop (26), and oats (28).

3.4 Crop classification
The classifier predicts the current year’s CDL class for a cell from four categories: corn (1), soybean (5), winter wheat
(24), and Other, which combines all remaining selected CDL codes (0-61). Winter wheat serves as the required third
crop class, and Other ensures the classifier must also recognize cells that are none of the three. Because the NDVI
features summarize the season through its late weeks, this is an end-of-season mapping task rather than a pre-planting
forecast.
The classification mask retained cells with a selected CDL code in at least three years during 2013-2022, separately
from the rotation analysis’s eligibility rule. Each observation is one cell in one year. Corn and soybean dominate
the Corn Belt, so a random sample would be mostly those two crops and a classifier could score well while largely
ignoring winter wheat. To prevent this, we sampled 125,000 observations per class per year. This gave 4.5 million
training observations from 2013-2021, 500,000 validation observations from 2022, and 500,000 test observations from
6

2023. The consequence is the “true” crop for each observation is its CDL label, which is itself a satellite-based estimate
with its own errors, so our accuracy measures how often the classifier agrees with the CDL, not how often it matches
what was actually planted. Also, because each class makes up a quarter of the sample, each class counts equally
toward overall accuracy. However, balancing only changes what we evaluate on, not the landscape the classifier would
be applied to. If we used it to map every cell in the Corn Belt, most of those cells would be corn or soybean, so the
map’s overall accuracy would depend mostly on how well the classifier handles those two crops, and less on winter
wheat.
The full model uses 38 features in three groups, one per data stream (full list in Appendix C):
• CDL history (19) describes what the cell grew before: the crops of the five preceding years, how often it switched
or repeated crops, how long since it last grew corn or soybean, the sequence metrics of Section 3.2, and what its
immediate neighbors grew.
• NDVI (15) describes the current season’s curve: its base, peak, and amplitude, the timing of green-up and
peak, and its average level in the early, middle, and late season. These are computed directly from the weekly
observations, not from the fitted curves of Section 3.1.
• SMAP (4) describes the season’s moisture: spring and growing-season averages, and how often the cell was
unusually wet or dry.
We used a LightGBM, a gradient-boosted decision tree implementation designed for large tabular datasets [3]. Tree
ensembles suit this problem because the relationships we expect are nonlinear and interaction-heavy - e.g., the meaning
of a late NDVI peak depends on last year’s crop - and LightGBM handles missing values natively, which mattered
because SMAP features are missing for the 2013-2014 training rows. We used early stopping on the 2022 validation
set to decide how many trees to grow (hyperparameters in Appendix C). To measure what each data source adds, we
trained four configurations on the same training and validation years: CDL history only, CDL plus NDVI, CDL plus
SMAP, and all three. The comparison was made on the 2022 validation sample. We then evaluated the full model on
2023.

The regular share depends heavily on how strictly “regular” is defined, which is observed in Figure 3b. Relaxing
the alternation threshold from 0.70 to 0.50 and allowing six mismatches instead of three more than doubles it, from

and the alternation axis are independent of each other, and the large irregular class is where the definitional uncertainty
lives.
Since about two-thirds of eligible cells are labeled irregular, it is worth being clear about what that label means. An
irregular history can reflect a repeated multi-crop sequence - e.g., corn-corn-soybean - an interruption by another crop,
or an error in one year’s CDL label; it does not by itself indicate poor management.

The transition matrix in Figure 4 shows which of these departures are most common. And we can see that switching
is the most likely move from either main crop: a corn year is followed by soybean 55.4% of the time, and a soybean
year by corn 60.0% of the time. However, neither switch is close to certain - corn is followed by corn 31% of the time,
compared with 24% for soybean following soybean, so corn is the crop more likely to be planted two years in a row.
This is the main reason soybean→corn is the more reliable of the two switches (1.08 times as likely as corn→soybean).
Corn and soybean years are also followed by another crop 14% and 16% of the time, respectively, and about one in five
pairs (19.3%) starts from a year of another crop. Once a cell is in another crop, there is no strong pull in any direction:
the next year is corn, soybean, or another crop with similar probabilities (0.34, 0.30, and 0.36). Every pair that is not a
corn-soybean switch is therefore either a repeat, more often of corn than of soybean, or a move into or out of another
crop, and these are the moves that push a cell’s history away from regular rotation. Because the matrix looks back only
one year, it cannot separate a deliberate corn-corn-soybean rotation from corn that is repeated irregularly, since both
appear as a mix of corn→corn and corn→soybean pairs.
Spatially, we can see in Figure 3a, that regular rotation is concentrated in a continuous band through the center of the
footprint, while irregular histories dominate its northern portion and outer margins, and monoculture appears mostly
in clusters along the western and southern edges. We did not formally test these patterns against county boundaries,
surface water, or soils, so we can describe them only visually.

5 Discussion
This work used three CropSmart data products, the CDL, MODIS NDVI, and SMAP soil moisture, to work through
the four tasks of the NAFSI Track 1 challenge across the Corn Belt. We characterized the seasonal NDVI curves of
corn, soybean, and winter wheat with an HSGP, and found that the crops are separated by when they peak rather than
how green they get. We identified regular corn-soybean rotation from ten-year CDL histories with a transparent rule,
and found that the share of regular rotation ranges from about 28% to 60% depending on how strictly “regular” is
defined. We scored soil moisture against a 2015-2021 baseline with a conjugate Bayesian model that accounts for
the short SMAP record, which showed broadly wet conditions in spring 2019 and drier, more uneven conditions in
summer 2022. And we trained a LightGBM classifier on CDL history, NDVI, and SMAP features that reached 79.2%
accuracy on the 2023 test year, with NDVI adding to what crop history alone provides and SMAP adding almost
nothing.
6 Limitations
Despite these results, our implementation still has several limitations worth acknowledging.
Every unit in this study is coarser than an individual field. The phenology curves average many fields into one
regional mean per week, so they describe the typical season rather than the range of seasons among fields; the shaded
bands in Figure 2 represent variation in regional means, not field-to-field spread - NDVI pixels at 250 m often contain
more than one crop, and moisture cells share a single 9 km value. Above all, every label in this study comes from
the CDL, which has errors of its own. A single mislabeled year can move a cell between rotation classes, and the
classifier’s accuracy measures agreement with the CDL, not with what was actually planted.
Pooling 18 years into one curve per crop treats every year as a draw around the same season. The pooled curve
therefore averages over planting-date shifts that are themselves informative, and the model cannot tell us how much
the season moves from year to year.
There is no ground truth for which fields are “regular,” and our headline share of 27% depends on thresholds
we chose; under looser but still reasonable definitions, it reaches 60%. The three-class scheme also treats any non-
alternating sequence as irregular, so common structured rotations such as corn-corn-soybean or wheat-soybean double
cropping are grouped with genuinely erratic histories.
With SMAP beginning in 2015, each cell-week has at most seven reference years. The NIG model accounts for this
uncertainty, but it cannot create the long climatology that a robust anomaly needs. Including 2019 in its own reference
also dampens that event’s anomaly, as seen in the much smaller share of extreme 2019 observations. We also have no
independent observations of flood extent, drought severity, or crop damage, so the moisture scores describe unusual
conditions rather than validated detections of agricultural impact.
Balanced sampling ensures that every class is evaluated fairly, but it also means our accuracy does not reflect a
map in which corn and soybean cover far more area than wheat. An area-weighted evaluation would likely produce a
different overall accuracy and different error patterns.
7 Suggested Future Work
The most direct improvement to Task 1 would be to replace the pooled curve with a hierarchical GP, in which each
year’s curve is a deviation from a shared crop-level curve. This would let the model learn how much the season shifts
from year to year, and turn peak timing into a per-year estimate with its own uncertainty, which could then be related
14

to spring moisture or temperature. Because the HSGP scales linearly with N , the same structure could be extended
to individual pixels or counties. Comparing the GP against a parametric model, would also show whether the added
flexibility is earned.
As an extension of the rule-based classes, we also computed a Dirichlet-Multinomial posterior for each cell’s
transition probabilities, giving a continuous posterior probability of regular rotation and its uncertainty. However, we
did not use it in the reported classification. Mapping that probability, rather than hard classes, would directly show
which cells are confidently regular and which sit near the boundary, which is exactly where the sensitivity analysis
shows the shares move. Extending the classes to recognize structured multi-crop rotations, and using field boundaries
such as the USDA Crop Sequence Boundaries in place of grid cells, would make the results interpretable at the level
where rotation decisions are actually made.
The challenge asks whether rotation regularity is associated with county boundaries, proximity to water, or soil
type. Joining the class map to county polygons, hydrography, a soils database, and irrigation extent, and modeling the
probability of regular rotation as a function of these covariates, would turn the visual patterns in Figure 3a into a tested
result.
For prospective use, the reference period should exclude the event year - e.g., through a leave-one- year-out clima-
tology. A longer reference could come from a reanalysis product such as ERA5, which extends decades before SMAP,
and SMAP L4’s root-zone layer may better reflect the water available to established crops than the surface layer. Most
importantly, the anomalies should be validated against independent outcomes such as drought monitor categories, crop
condition reports, or prevented-planting acreage.
